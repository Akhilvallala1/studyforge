# From the Struggle for Existence to Variation

## The question that opens the chapter

Darwin begins Chapter IV not with an assertion but with two questions:

> "How will the struggle for existence, discussed too briefly in the last chapter, act in regard to variation? Can the principle of selection, which we have seen is so potent in the hands of man, apply in nature?"

Notice what each question does. The first links two things the reader has already been given separately — the *struggle for existence* (Chapter III) and *variation* (the earlier chapters, especially variation under domestication). Darwin's whole argument depends on connecting them: variation supplies differences, the struggle decides which differences matter.

The second question is the analogical one. Human breeders select; that power Darwin has already shown to be "so potent in the hands of man." Can something like it operate without a breeder?

His answer is immediate and confident, but carefully phrased as an expectation rather than a proof: "I think we shall see that it can act most effectually." The rest of the chapter is the demonstration; the opening only states the destination.

## The three things to "bear in mind"

Before arguing, Darwin asks the reader to hold three facts in view. They are the premises the argument will lean on.

1. **The extent of variation.** Domestic productions vary "in an endless number of strange peculiarities," and organisms under nature vary too — though, Darwin is careful to add, *in a lesser degree*. He does not claim wild species are as variable as pigeons or cabbages.
2. **The strength of heredity.** Variation would be useless to selection if offspring did not resemble parents; Darwin notes "how strong the hereditary tendency is." Under domestication, he says, "the whole organisation becomes in some degree plastic."
3. **The complexity of relations.** The "mutual relations of all organic beings to each other and to their physical conditions of life" are "infinitely complex and close-fitting." This matters because it means there are countless ways in which a slight difference could turn out to be useful.

## The step from useful-to-man to useful-to-the-being

The pivot of the passage is a shift in *who* the variation is useful to. Breeders have unquestionably found variations useful to themselves. Darwin asks whether it is improbable that variations useful "in some way to each being in the great and complex battle of life" should also occur — "sometimes," and "in the course of thousands of generations."

The modesty of that claim is deliberate. He does not need useful variations to be common; he needs them to occur occasionally over vast stretches of time.

If they do occur, the struggle for existence supplies the rest. Remembering "that many more individuals are born than can possibly survive," individuals with "any advantage, however slight, over others, would have the best chance of surviving and of procreating their kind." Conversely, "any variation in the least degree injurious would be rigidly destroyed."

## The definition

Darwin then names the process:

> "This preservation of favourable variations and the rejection of injurious variations, I call Natural Selection."

Two halves, not one: preservation *and* rejection. The term is a coinage he is introducing, defined by what it does rather than by any agency behind it.

## The third case: neutral variations

A detail easily skipped over, but revealing. Variations "neither useful nor injurious would not be affected by natural selection, and would be left a fluctuating element, as perhaps we see in the species called polymorphic."

So Darwin's mechanism is explicitly *not* a claim that every character of every organism is adaptive. Where a variation makes no difference to survival, natural selection has no grip on it, and the character is free to fluctuate. Polymorphic species — those showing several forms — are offered, tentatively ("perhaps"), as what this looks like in nature.

## Summary of the argument's shape

- Organisms vary, and variations are inherited.
- Relations among organisms are so complex and close-fitting that some variations will happen to be useful to their possessors.
- More individuals are born than can survive.
- Therefore the slightly advantaged survive and reproduce; the slightly disadvantaged are destroyed.
- That two-sided process is Natural Selection — and it leaves neutral variations untouched.

# Variability and Heredity as Preconditions

Before Darwin can argue that natural selection *works*, he has to secure a handful of premises. Chapter IV opens not with an assertion but with a request: "Let it be borne in mind..." He is asking the reader to hold several facts in view at once, because the argument for natural selection is a chain, and each of these facts is a link.

## The opening question

The chapter begins by connecting back to the struggle for existence:

> "How will the struggle for existence, discussed too briefly in the last chapter, act in regard to variation? Can the principle of selection, which we have seen is so potent in the hands of man, apply in nature? I think we shall see that it can act most effectually."

Note the structure. Man's selection has already been established as potent — that was the work of the earlier chapters on domestication. The question now is whether the same principle can operate without a selector. Everything that follows is an attempt to show that the raw materials selection needs are present in nature too.

## Premise 1: Variation is abundant and strange

> "Let it be borne in mind in what an endless number of strange peculiarities our domestic productions, and, in a lesser degree, those under nature, vary."

Two things deserve attention here.

First, the *quantity*: "an endless number." Darwin is not claiming that variation is occasional or confined to a few obvious traits like size or colour. It is inexhaustible in its directions.

Second, the *comparison*: domestic productions vary in this way, and wild organisms do so **in a lesser degree**. This is a careful, honest concession. Darwin does not pretend that nature is as variable as the pigeon loft or the cabbage patch. He concedes that variation under nature is less, but insists it is real. The argument does not need wild variation to match domestic variation — it only needs some.

The word "strange" is doing work too. The peculiarities are not tidy or predictable. Variation throws up oddities, and this matters later: selection does not need to order up the variation it uses, it only needs a supply of miscellaneous differences to sift.

## Premise 2: The hereditary tendency is strong

> "...and how strong the hereditary tendency is."

Variation alone would be useless. If an advantageous peculiarity died with the individual that bore it, no amount of differential survival could accumulate anything. Darwin's phrase is a *tendency* rather than a law — he is not claiming that offspring are copies of their parents, only that the pull toward resemblance is strong. That is exactly as much as the argument requires: enough transmission for advantages to be handed down across generations.

Variation supplies the differences; heredity preserves them. Neither premise works without the other.

## Premise 3: Under domestication, the whole organisation becomes plastic

> "Under domestication, it may be truly said that the whole organisation becomes in some degree plastic."

This is a strong claim, and it is worth being precise about its scope. Darwin says *the whole organisation* — not just superficial features, but the organism throughout. And he says *in some degree* plastic — a hedge that keeps the claim modest in intensity while keeping it broad in extent.

The importance of this is that it rules out a natural objection: that variation touches only trivia while the deep structure of an organism is fixed. If that were true, selection could rearrange surfaces but never build anything substantial. Darwin's claim of general plasticity says there is no protected core immune to change.

## Premise 4: Relations among beings are complex and close-fitting

> "Let it be borne in mind how infinitely complex and close-fitting are the mutual relations of all organic beings to each other and to their physical conditions of life."

This premise concerns not the organism but its world. Each being stands in intricate relation to other beings and to its physical conditions. Because the fit is so close, even a slight change in an organism can matter to its success. A world of loose, forgiving relationships would make small variations irrelevant; a close-fitting world makes them consequential.

## Assembling the argument

With the premises in hand, Darwin poses his question as a challenge:

> "Can it, then, be thought improbable, seeing that variations useful to man have undoubtedly occurred, that other variations useful in some way to each being in the great and complex battle of life, should sometimes occur in the course of thousands of generations?"

The move is an analogy plus a shift of beneficiary. Variations useful **to man** have undoubtedly occurred — that is a matter of record, not speculation. Why should variations useful **to the organism itself** not also occur? And note the timescale: "in the course of thousands of generations." Such variations need not be common. They need only *sometimes* occur.

The next step invokes the struggle for existence:

> "If such do occur, can we doubt (remembering that many more individuals are born than can possibly survive) that individuals having any advantage, however slight, over others, would have the best chance of surviving and of procreating their kind?"

The parenthesis is the engine. Because more are born than can survive, a slight advantage translates into a better *chance* — Darwin says chance, not certainty. And survival is not enough on its own: he specifies surviving **and procreating**, since a variation that is not passed on leads nowhere.

## The definition, and its third case

> "On the other hand, we may feel sure that any variation in the least degree injurious would be rigidly destroyed. This preservation of favourable variations and the rejection of injurious variations, I call Natural Selection."

Selection is thus two-sided: it preserves and it rejects. But Darwin immediately adds a third possibility that is easy to overlook:

> "Variations neither useful nor injurious would not be affected by natural selection, and would be left a fluctuating element, as perhaps we see in the species called polymorphic."

Natural selection is not a force acting on all variation whatsoever. Neutral variation slips through untouched and remains "a fluctuating element" — and Darwin offers polymorphic species, tentatively ("as perhaps"), as a possible instance of this.

## Why the premises matter

Strip out any one premise and the argument fails:

- No variation → nothing to select from.
- No heredity → nothing accumulates.
- No plasticity of the whole organisation → only trivial change is possible.
- No close-fitting relations → slight differences would not matter.
- No excess of births over survivors → no differential survival.

Darwin's rhetorical strategy is to make each premise something the reader already grants — facts from the breeder's yard and from ordinary natural history — so that natural selection arrives less as a hypothesis than as an inference the reader can hardly refuse.

# The Web of Mutual Relations

## Setting up the question

Chapter IV of the *Origin* opens with a question, not an answer:

> "How will the struggle for existence, discussed too briefly in the last chapter, act in regard to variation? Can the principle of selection, which we have seen is so potent in the hands of man, apply in nature?"

Darwin has already shown what breeders can do. The problem is whether nature has anything that plays the breeder's part. His answer is built out of several things he asks the reader to "bear in mind," and the third of them is the subject of this lesson.

## The three things to bear in mind

1. **Organisms vary in an "endless number of strange peculiarities."** This is most obvious under domestication, and true "in a lesser degree" of organisms in nature. Note the asymmetry: Darwin does not pretend wild variation is as luxuriant as barnyard variation.
2. **Heredity is strong.** Peculiarities are passed on; "how strong the hereditary tendency is." Under domestication, he says, "the whole organisation becomes in some degree plastic" — the organism is not a rigid type but something that bends.
3. **"How infinitely complex and close-fitting are the mutual relations of all organic beings to each other and to their physical conditions of life."**

The third point is the one that turns the first two into an argument.

## Unpacking "infinitely complex and close-fitting"

Two distinct claims are packed into that phrase, and they do different work.

**Complex** means that every organism stands in relations to a huge number of other things: to the beings it eats, to the beings that eat it, to competitors for the same food, to whatever pollinates or disperses or parasitises it — *and* to the physical conditions, heat, drought, soil, season. Darwin deliberately joins the two kinds of relation with an "and": relations to each other **and** to their physical conditions of life. Climate alone is not the whole story; neither is the rest of the living world.

**Close-fitting** means these relations are tight. Small differences matter. If the fit between a being and its circumstances were loose and sloppy, a slight change in beak, leg, root, or coat would make no difference to anything, and there would be nothing for selection to bite on.

Put the two together and you get the point Darwin needs: because the relations are *many*, there are enormous numbers of ways in which a chance peculiarity could turn out to matter; because they are *close*, even very small peculiarities can matter.

## The argument from man's selection to nature's

Having laid down the three points, Darwin asks a rhetorical question:

> "Can it, then, be thought improbable, seeing that variations useful to man have undoubtedly occurred, that other variations useful in some way to each being in the great and complex battle of life, should sometimes occur in the course of thousands of generations?"

The logic is worth slowing down over. The premise is not that useful variations *must* occur in nature — that would beg the question. The premise is a fact nobody disputes: variations useful *to man* have undoubtedly occurred, since breeders have found and used them. Darwin then asks why the same stock of variability, playing into a far richer set of relations, should never happen to throw up something useful *to the organism itself*. The web of mutual relations is what makes that a modest expectation rather than a wild hope: with so many close-fitting relations to be slightly improved, and with "thousands of generations" to work in, the chance of never once hitting a useful variation looks small.

Notice also "in some way" and "the great and complex battle of life." Usefulness is not restricted to some obvious virtue like strength. Anything that improves the fit anywhere in the web counts.

## From plausible variation to natural selection

Once a useful variation is granted, the rest follows quickly, and Darwin reminds us of the arithmetic from the previous chapter:

> "If such do occur, can we doubt (remembering that many more individuals are born than can possibly survive) that individuals having any advantage, however slight, over others, would have the best chance of surviving and of procreating their kind?"

The superabundance of births is what converts a slight advantage into a real difference in survival: since most must die anyway, who dies is not arbitrary. And the mirror case: "any variation in the least degree injurious would be rigidly destroyed."

This two-sided process is what he names: "This preservation of favourable variations and the rejection of injurious variations, I call Natural Selection." Selection is a name for an outcome of the web plus the surplus of births — not an extra force added to them.

## The neglected third case

Darwin adds a qualification that is easy to skip and reveals the shape of the theory:

> "Variations neither useful nor injurious would not be affected by natural selection, and would be left a fluctuating element, as perhaps we see in the species called polymorphic."

Natural selection is not a universal solvent. Where a variation makes no difference to the fit, selection has no grip on it, and the character is free to fluctuate. Darwin offers polymorphic species — those found in several forms at once — as a possible example. So the close-fitting web does not imply that *every* feature of every organism is finely adjusted; it implies only that where fit matters, differences in fit will be sorted.

## Why this lesson matters for the rest of the chapter

Everything later in Chapter IV — selection acting on characters of trifling importance, at all ages, on both sexes, divergence of character — depends on this opening picture. A world of loose, simple relations would give natural selection almost nothing to do. A world of infinitely complex, close-fitting relations gives it work at every point, including points that look trivial to us.

# Advantage, However Slight

In the opening pages of Chapter IV of *On the Origin of Species*, Darwin does something that looks almost too simple to be a scientific revolution: he lays out a few observations that no naturalist of his time would have disputed, and then draws a conclusion from them. The power of the argument lies not in any single premise but in the way they lock together. Let us walk the chain link by link.

## The question being asked

Darwin opens by naming the problem: "How will the struggle for existence, discussed too briefly in the last chapter, act in regard to variation? Can the principle of selection, which we have seen is so potent in the hands of man, apply in nature?"

Notice the shape of the question. Two things have already been established in earlier chapters — that organisms vary, and that they struggle for existence — and Darwin now asks what happens when you put them in the same room. He also has a model in mind: the breeder. Human selection is already known to be potent. The question is whether nature can do the same kind of work without a selector.

His answer, stated up front: "I think we shall see that it can act most effectually."

## The premises

Darwin asks the reader to "bear in mind" three things before the argument proper begins.

**1. Variation is abundant.** Our domestic productions vary in "an endless number of strange peculiarities," and those under nature vary too, though "in a lesser degree." This ordering matters: Darwin does not overstate his case. Wild variation is real but less florid than the variation breeders work with.

**2. Variation is inherited.** "How strong the hereditary tendency is." Without heredity, an advantageous peculiarity would die with its possessor and nothing would accumulate. Under domestication, he adds, "it may be truly said that the whole organisation becomes in some degree plastic" — nearly every part of the body can be shifted.

**3. Life is a web of close-fitting relations.** "How infinitely complex and close-fitting are the mutual relations of all organic beings to each other and to their physical conditions of life." This premise does quiet but essential work. In a world of tight interlocking relations, tiny differences matter. A slightly longer beak, a slightly earlier flowering, a slightly better tolerance of drought can tip an outcome, because the fit between organism and circumstance is already so close that small adjustments are consequential.

## The bridge: from useful-to-man to useful-to-the-being

Here is the pivot of the passage. Darwin writes:

> "Can it, then, be thought improbable, seeing that variations useful to man have undoubtedly occurred, that other variations useful in some way to each being in the great and complex battle of life, should sometimes occur in the course of thousands of generations?"

The move is modest and therefore hard to resist. Variations useful *to man* have "undoubtedly occurred" — every breed of pigeon and every strain of wheat is evidence. Is it really improbable that variations useful *to the organism itself* should also turn up? Darwin does not claim they are common. He asks only for "sometimes," and only "in the course of thousands of generations." The argument is built to run on a very small supply of raw material, because time is doing much of the work.

## The conclusion

Grant that such variations occur, and add one more fact — the Malthusian fact from the previous chapter:

> "If such do occur, can we doubt (remembering that many more individuals are born than can possibly survive) that individuals having any advantage, however slight, over others, would have the best chance of surviving and of procreating their kind?"

The parenthesis is the engine. If every individual born could survive and breed, an advantage would confer nothing; there would be no filter for it to pass through. It is precisely because most must die that any difference in the odds of dying becomes consequential.

Read the wording carefully. Darwin says such individuals "would have the best chance" — not that they will certainly survive. This is a statement about probabilities, not destinies. The lucky and the unlucky exist; the claim is only that the odds are tilted. And the phrase "surviving and of procreating their kind" contains both halves of what matters: bare survival is not enough, the advantage must be passed on.

The word "slight" is deliberate too. Darwin is not appealing to dramatic monstrosities or sudden new forms. Advantages so small as to be nearly invisible are sufficient, because they are tested over thousands of generations and over enormous numbers of individuals.

## The other side of the ledger

Selection is not only a preserver. Darwin turns immediately to the negative case:

> "On the other hand, we may feel sure that any variation in the least degree injurious would be rigidly destroyed."

The language stiffens here. Where the favourable case is stated as a question we can hardly doubt, the injurious case is one we "may feel sure" of, and the destruction is "rigid." Nature is stricter about weeding out than about promoting.

## The definition

Only now, with both halves in place, does the term arrive:

> "This preservation of favourable variations and the rejection of injurious variations, I call Natural Selection."

Note what the definition includes. Natural selection is *not* just survival of the fit; it is a two-sided process — preservation **and** rejection. And it is a name given to a consequence of the preceding argument, not an independent force introduced to explain things. Darwin has earned the term before using it.

## The third category: variations that don't matter

Darwin closes the passage with an important qualification that is easy to skip:

> "Variations neither useful nor injurious would not be affected by natural selection, and would be left a fluctuating element, as perhaps we see in the species called polymorphic."

This saves the theory from a common misreading. Natural selection does not sculpt every feature of every organism. A variation that makes no difference to survival is invisible to selection; it is neither kept nor destroyed but left to drift as "a fluctuating element." Darwin suggests we may see exactly this in **polymorphic** species — those that persist with several forms at once, with no one form displacing the others.

## The chain, in brief

1. Organisms vary, and variations are inherited.
2. The relations of organisms to each other and to their conditions are complex and close-fitting, so small differences tell.
3. Since variations useful to man occur, variations useful to the organism must sometimes occur too, given thousands of generations.
4. Far more individuals are born than can survive.
5. Therefore individuals with any advantage, however slight, have the best chance of surviving and of procreating their kind.
6. Conversely, any variation in the least degree injurious would be rigidly destroyed.
7. Preservation of the favourable plus rejection of the injurious = **Natural Selection**.
8. Variations that are neither remain untouched, a fluctuating element.

Each step is small. Taken together, over thousands of generations, they are enough.

# Preservation and Rejection: The Formal Definition

## The sentence that names the principle

At the heart of the opening pages of Chapter IV, Darwin stops arguing and gives his idea a name:

> "This preservation of favourable variations and the rejection of injurious variations, I call Natural Selection."

Notice how the sentence is built. Darwin does not say "natural selection is the survival of the fittest," nor "natural selection is a force," nor "nature chooses." He points backwards — *this* — at a process he has just described, and then attaches a label to it. The definition is therefore only as good as the description that precedes it, so we have to read the run-up carefully before we can read the definition itself.

## The run-up: four things Darwin asks you to bear in mind

Before the naming sentence, Darwin lays out the ingredients, mostly as things to "bear in mind" from earlier chapters:

1. **Variation is abundant.** Domestic productions vary "in what an endless number of strange peculiarities," and those under nature vary too, though "in a lesser degree." Under domestication, he says, "the whole organisation becomes in some degree plastic."
2. **Heredity is strong.** "How strong the hereditary tendency is" — variations are passed on, so any advantage is not lost when its possessor dies.
3. **Relations among organisms are intricate.** The "mutual relations of all organic beings to each other and to their physical conditions of life" are "infinitely complex and close-fitting." This matters because it means there are countless small ways a variation could turn out to be useful.
4. **More are born than can survive.** This is carried over from the struggle for existence: "many more individuals are born than can possibly survive."

From these, Darwin draws his conclusion as a pair of rhetorical questions and one flat assertion. If variations useful to man have undoubtedly occurred, is it improbable that variations "useful in some way to each being in the great and complex battle of life" should sometimes occur over thousands of generations? And if they do, can we doubt that individuals with "any advantage, however slight, over others, would have the best chance of surviving and of procreating their kind"? Then the other half: "we may feel sure that any variation in the least degree injurious would be rigidly destroyed."

## Taking the definition term by term

**"Preservation"** — not creation. Natural selection does not make the favourable variation; it keeps one that has already appeared. Darwin's whole argument depends on variation arising first, from causes he treats separately. Selection is a filter, not a source.

**"Favourable"** — favourable *to the being that has it*, in its own circumstances. Darwin's phrasing is "useful in some way to each being in the great and complex battle of life." There is no general standard of improvement here; usefulness is relative to a particular organism in a particular web of relations.

**"Rejection"** — the definition has two halves, and the second is often forgotten. Selection is as much about what is removed as about what is kept. Darwin's language for this side is much harsher and more certain: injurious variations "would be rigidly destroyed," and he says we may "feel sure" of it, whereas on the favourable side he speaks only of the "best chance" of surviving and procreating.

**"Injurious"** — and note the qualifier: "in the least degree injurious." Just as an advantage "however slight" counts on the positive side, a disadvantage of the smallest degree counts on the negative side. Darwin's principle is deliberately sensitive to tiny differences, because over thousands of generations tiny differences are what he has to work with.

**"Variations"** — the units being preserved or rejected are heritable differences between individuals, not the individuals themselves considered as wholes.

## The third category: variations that are neither

Darwin immediately closes off a misreading. His definition is exhaustive in an odd way — it covers favourable and injurious variations, and *says nothing about the rest*:

> "Variations neither useful nor injurious would not be affected by natural selection, and would be left a fluctuating element, as perhaps we see in the species called polymorphic."

This is an important limit on the theory. Natural selection is not a universal explanation for every trait an organism has. Where a variation makes no difference to the struggle for existence, the principle simply has no grip, and the character is left free to fluctuate. Darwin offers polymorphic species — species that occur in several forms at once — as a possible visible result.

## Why call it "selection"?

The word is borrowed, and Darwin borrows it openly. His framing question at the head of the chapter is: "Can the principle of selection, which we have seen is so potent in the hands of man, apply in nature?" He has already spent chapters on what breeders do — accumulating slight differences by choosing which individuals leave offspring — and he now proposes that nature does the analogous thing.

The analogy does real argumentative work. Darwin's warrant for believing that useful variations occur in the wild is that "variations useful to man have undoubtedly occurred" under domestication. If the raw material exists for the breeder, it is not improbable that raw material useful to the organism itself exists too. The chapter's own summary line — "Natural Selection: its power compared with man's selection" — announces that the comparison structures the whole chapter.

The strain in the metaphor is obvious, and worth naming. A human selector has intentions and picks deliberately; in Darwin's definition nothing picks. Preservation is simply what happens when the advantaged individual has "the best chance of surviving and of procreating," and rejection is what happens when the disadvantaged one is destroyed. The word "natural" in "Natural Selection" is doing the job of removing the chooser while keeping the outcome.

## Reading the definition as a claim you could test

Stripped down, the named principle asserts:

- heritable variation exists;
- more individuals are born than can survive;
- therefore the differentially advantageous variations tend to be kept and the disadvantageous ones tend to be destroyed;
- neutral ones are untouched.

Everything Darwin builds afterwards — action on characters of trifling importance, at all ages and on both sexes, divergence of character, extinction — is an extension of this one compact definition.

# The Neutral Case and Polymorphic Species

## Darwin's definition has two halves — and one remainder

At the end of the opening passage of Chapter IV, Darwin gives his famous definition:

> "This preservation of favourable variations and the rejection of injurious variations, I call Natural Selection."

Notice that the definition is built out of two verbs pointing in opposite directions. On one side, individuals "having any advantage, however slight, over others, would have the best chance of surviving and of procreating their kind." On the other, "any variation in the least degree injurious would be rigidly destroyed." Preservation and rejection: a sieve with an upper and a lower action.

But a sieve sorted by usefulness has to leave something behind. What about a variation that is *neither* useful nor injurious? Darwin adds the qualification immediately, in a single sentence:

> "Variations neither useful nor injurious would not be affected by natural selection, and would be left a fluctuating element, as perhaps we see in the species called polymorphic."

This sentence is short, but it does a great deal of work, and it is worth slowing down over.

## Why the neutral case follows necessarily

The neutral case is not an exception grafted onto the theory. It is forced by the theory's own logic.

Darwin's engine runs on differential survival. More individuals are born than can possibly survive; among the crowd of competitors, the ones with an edge have "the best chance" of living to reproduce. Everything the mechanism does, it does by way of that difference in chances. So if a variation makes no difference to a being's prospects in "the great and complex battle of life," the mechanism has nothing to grip. Natural selection cannot preserve it, because there is no advantage to be preserved; it cannot reject it, because there is nothing injurious to destroy. The variation is simply invisible to the process.

This matters for how you read the rest of the *Origin*. Darwin is **not** claiming that every character of every organism is there because it was useful. He is claiming that natural selection acts on the useful and the injurious, and he explicitly leaves a category outside its reach.

## "Left a fluctuating element"

The phrase Darwin chooses for what happens to neutral variation is *left a fluctuating element*. Two things are packed into it.

- **Left.** Not removed, not fixed, not tidied up. Selection passes over it and it remains in the species.
- **Fluctuating.** It is not held steady in any one form. Where a useful character would tend to be preserved and made regular, an indifferent one is free to wobble — to keep varying from individual to individual, generation to generation.

So the prediction is a specific one: in nature we should expect to find characters that vary a great deal and yet show no sign of being pinned down by advantage.

## Polymorphic species as the possible evidence

Darwin then points at a class of cases: "as perhaps we see in the species called polymorphic." *Polymorphic* means many-formed; these are the species naturalists had labelled as presenting several forms at once, resisting neat description because their members refuse to look alike. Darwin offers them as the place where his fluctuating element may actually be on display: variability that persists because nothing is sorting it.

Read the sentence carefully and you will see three signs of caution:

1. **"perhaps"** — Darwin proposes the connection, he does not assert it. He is suggesting an explanation for a known puzzle, not claiming to have proved it.
2. **"as perhaps we *see*"** — the polymorphic species are brought in as an observation that fits, an illustration of the expected pattern.
3. **"the species *called* polymorphic"** — he is borrowing a term other naturalists already used for a group of troublesome cases, rather than coining a category to suit himself.

This is a good miniature of Darwin's habit throughout the chapter: state the mechanism firmly, then attach the empirical illustration with a hedge.

## A worked contrast

Imagine three variations arising in a population, in the same run of "thousands of generations":

| Variation | Effect on the being's chances | Fate under natural selection |
|---|---|---|
| A slight advantage in the battle of life | Better chance of surviving and procreating | Preserved |
| Injurious "in the least degree" | Worse chance | "Rigidly destroyed" |
| Neither useful nor injurious | No difference to chances | Not affected; left fluctuating |

The third row is the whole of this lesson. Note also how low Darwin sets the thresholds in the first two rows: "any advantage, however slight" is enough to be picked up, and injury "in the least degree" is enough to be eliminated. Precisely because the boundaries are drawn so tightly around usefulness, the leftover category is defined sharply too — it is the strictly indifferent variation, not merely the slightly beneficial one.

## Why the qualification strengthens the argument

A reader might expect Darwin to want the widest possible claim for his new principle. He does the opposite here, and it pays. By marking out what natural selection does *not* touch, he makes the principle testable rather than all-purpose, and at the same time he converts an awkward fact — species that vary wildly and messily — into something his theory anticipates. The polymorphic species stop being a difficulty and become, perhaps, a visible remainder left by the sieve.

# Scope and Reach of Selection

## The chapter's own table of contents

Darwin opens Chapter IV with a compressed list of everything the chapter will cover. It reads almost like a syllabus:

> Natural Selection: its power compared with man's selection, its power on characters of trifling importance, its power at all ages and on both sexes. Sexual Selection. On the generality of intercrosses between individuals of the same species. Circumstances favourable and unfavourable to Natural Selection, namely, intercrossing, isolation, number of individuals. Slow action. Extinction caused by Natural Selection. Divergence of Character... Explains the Grouping of all organic beings.

The first four items in that list are all claims about **scope**—about how far the principle reaches. Before Darwin argues that natural selection *works*, he tells us how widely he intends to claim it works:

1. **Compared with man's selection.** Natural selection is to be measured against the deliberate breeding practised by humans, the subject of his earlier chapters. The comparison is the engine of the whole argument.
2. **On characters of trifling importance.** Not only on obviously vital organs, but on features that look too small to matter.
3. **At all ages.** Not only on adults; on any stage of life at which a variation appears.
4. **On both sexes.** And—listed separately, as its own heading—**Sexual Selection**, a distinct mode of selection that Darwin will treat in its own right.

The rest of the list moves from scope to conditions and consequences: what circumstances help or hinder selection (intercrossing, isolation, number of individuals), how slowly it acts, the extinction it causes, and finally divergence of character, which is meant to explain the grouping of all organic beings.

## From man's selection to nature's

Darwin's first move is to pose the question directly:

> How will the struggle for existence... act in regard to variation? Can the principle of selection, which we have seen is so potent in the hands of man, apply in nature?

His answer—"I think we shall see that it can act most effectually"—is then supported by a chain of things he asks the reader to "bear in mind."

**Step 1: Organisms vary widely.** Our domestic productions vary in "an endless number of strange peculiarities," and those under nature vary too, though "in a lesser degree." Note the asymmetry: Darwin does not claim wild variation is as abundant as domestic variation, only that it exists. Under domestication, he says, "the whole organisation becomes in some degree plastic."

**Step 2: Variation is inherited.** "How strong the hereditary tendency is." Without heredity, a favourable peculiarity would die with its possessor.

**Step 3: The relations of life are intricate.** The "mutual relations of all organic beings to each other and to their physical conditions of life" are "infinitely complex and close-fitting." This matters because a close-fitting system offers countless small ways in which a variation might be useful—there are far more openings for advantage than a simple picture of "survival in bad weather" would suggest.

**Step 4: Therefore useful variations should sometimes occur.** Variations useful *to man* have undoubtedly occurred, so it is not improbable that variations useful *to the organism itself*, in "the great and complex battle of life," should occur "in the course of thousands of generations."

**Step 5: The struggle does the sorting.** Remembering "that many more individuals are born than can possibly survive," any individual with an advantage, "however slight," would have the best chance of surviving and of leaving offspring. This is why a trifling advantage is enough: the surplus of births guarantees that most individuals must fail, so even a tiny edge in the odds is decisive over generations.

## The definition

Darwin then names the principle, and the naming is careful and two-sided:

> This preservation of favourable variations and the rejection of injurious variations, I call Natural Selection.

Selection is not only a saving of the good. It is equally a destruction of the bad: "any variation in the least degree injurious would be rigidly destroyed." *Rigidly*—not usually, not on average. Both halves belong to the definition.

## What selection does *not* touch

Immediately after defining his principle, Darwin limits it:

> Variations neither useful nor injurious would not be affected by natural selection, and would be left a fluctuating element, as perhaps we see in the species called polymorphic.

This is an important piece of intellectual honesty. Natural selection is not a universal explanation for every difference between organisms. A variation that makes no difference to survival is invisible to selection and simply drifts—"a fluctuating element"—and Darwin offers polymorphic species (those found in many varying forms) as a possible example of exactly this. Selection is powerful, and reaches even to trifling characters, but it reaches only as far as usefulness or harm reaches.

# Conditions Favourable and Unfavourable

## Reading a chapter by its own table of contents

Darwin opens Chapter IV with a compressed list of everything the chapter will argue. It is worth reading that list slowly, because it is Darwin's own map of the territory:

> Natural Selection: its power compared with man's selection, its power on characters of trifling importance, its power at all ages and on both sexes. Sexual Selection. On the generality of intercrosses between individuals of the same species. **Circumstances favourable and unfavourable to Natural Selection, namely, intercrossing, isolation, number of individuals. Slow action.** Extinction caused by Natural Selection. Divergence of Character... Action of Natural Selection, through Divergence of Character and Extinction, on the descendants from a common parent. Explains the Grouping of all organic beings.

This lesson concerns the bolded stretch and the item that leads into it.

## The four items, and how they are grouped

Notice the punctuation, because it does real work.

1. **"On the generality of intercrosses between individuals of the same species"** stands as a topic in its own right, listed after Sexual Selection and *before* the heading on circumstances. It is not itself named as one of the favourable or unfavourable circumstances; it is the preliminary question of how commonly, across organisms generally, two distinct individuals of a species cross.
2. **"Circumstances favourable and unfavourable to Natural Selection, namely, intercrossing, isolation, number of individuals."** The word *namely* tells us the list that follows is complete as far as this heading goes: three circumstances, no more. Darwin does not label any one of them as simply favourable or simply unfavourable — the heading covers both directions at once, so each of the three is to be weighed as helping in some situations and hindering in others.
3. **"Slow action."** This is given its own short heading, standing apart from the three circumstances rather than being one of them. Darwin evidently thought the slowness of the process needed separate emphasis.

A careful reader should hold this structure in mind and resist filling it in from memory or from later biology. The excerpt before us announces these topics; the arguments for them come further into the chapter. What we *can* do here is see how the opening paragraphs of the chapter already prepare the ground for them.

## The argument the headings rest on

Darwin's first substantive paragraph asks: "Can the principle of selection, which we have seen is so potent in the hands of man, apply in nature?" His answer, "I think we shall see that it can act most effectually," is built from a short chain of things he asks the reader to bear in mind:

- Organisms vary "in an endless number of strange peculiarities" — greatly under domestication, and "in a lesser degree" under nature.
- "The hereditary tendency is strong," so variations are passed on.
- Under domestication "the whole organisation becomes in some degree plastic."
- The "mutual relations of all organic beings to each other and to their physical conditions of life" are "infinitely complex and close-fitting."

From these he draws the inference: since variations useful to man have undoubtedly occurred, it is not improbable that variations useful to the being itself "should sometimes occur in the course of thousands of generations."

That phrase — *in the course of thousands of generations* — is the seed of the later heading "Slow action." Useful variations are not expected to appear on demand; they are expected to turn up occasionally, over spans of time measured in generations by the thousand. Whatever natural selection accomplishes, it accomplishes at that pace.

## The definition itself

The crucial premise for the next step is one Darwin puts in parentheses, as if it were already settled from Chapter III: "remembering that many more individuals are born than can possibly survive." Because of that surplus, an individual with "any advantage, however slight, over others, would have the best chance of surviving and of procreating their kind." And conversely: "any variation in the least degree injurious would be rigidly destroyed."

Then the definition:

> This preservation of favourable variations and the rejection of injurious variations, I call Natural Selection.

Note that it is defined as a two-sided process — preservation *and* rejection — not merely as the survival of the fit.

## The third case: variations that are neither

Darwin immediately adds a limit to his own principle. "Variations neither useful nor injurious would not be affected by natural selection, and would be left a fluctuating element, as perhaps we see in the species called polymorphic."

This matters for the topics of this lesson. Natural selection is not a force acting on every difference an organism displays; it is silent about differences that make no difference to survival, and such characters are left free to fluctuate. Polymorphic species — those presenting many variable forms — are offered, cautiously ("as perhaps we see"), as the visible result.

## What to carry forward

- Darwin names exactly three circumstances under the "favourable and unfavourable" heading: **intercrossing, isolation, number of individuals**.
- **Slow action** is emphasised separately, and the opening argument already invokes "thousands of generations."
- The **generality of intercrosses** is a prior question, treated on its own before the circumstances are weighed.
- Natural selection, as defined, both preserves and rejects — and does neither to variations that are useful in no way and injurious in no way.

# Extinction, Divergence, and the Grouping of Organic Beings

## Reading a chapter by its headnote

Darwin opens Chapter IV of *On the Origin of Species* not with an argument but with a list. Nineteenth-century books often carried such a headnote, a compressed table of the chapter's contents. It is worth reading slowly, because it tells us exactly where the chapter is going:

> Natural Selection: its power compared with man's selection, its power on characters of trifling importance, its power at all ages and on both sexes. Sexual Selection. On the generality of intercrosses between individuals of the same species. Circumstances favourable and unfavourable to Natural Selection, namely, intercrossing, isolation, number of individuals. Slow action. **Extinction caused by Natural Selection. Divergence of Character, related to the diversity of inhabitants of any small area, and to naturalisation. Action of Natural Selection, through Divergence of Character and Extinction, on the descendants from a common parent. Explains the Grouping of all organic beings.**

The bolded portion is the chapter's destination. Notice the shape of it. The first items in the list are about the *mechanism* of natural selection — how strong it is, what kinds of characters it touches, what conditions help or hinder it. The last items are about the mechanism's *consequences on a grand scale*: extinction, divergence, and finally the arrangement of all living things into groups.

This lesson looks at that destination in advance, so that the detailed arguments make sense when we meet them.

## The engine: what natural selection is

Before the grand consequences, Darwin needs the engine. He builds it in a single paragraph, as a chain of considerations he asks the reader to hold in mind at once:

1. **Organisms vary.** Domestic productions vary in "an endless number of strange peculiarities," and those under nature do too, though in a lesser degree. Under domestication "the whole organisation becomes in some degree plastic."
2. **Variation is inherited.** "How strong the hereditary tendency is."
3. **Life is intricately interconnected.** The mutual relations of all organic beings, to each other and to their physical conditions, are "infinitely complex and close-fitting."
4. **Therefore useful variations should sometimes arise.** Since variations useful *to man* have undoubtedly occurred, it can hardly be thought improbable that variations useful to the being itself, in "the great and complex battle of life," should sometimes occur "in the course of thousands of generations."
5. **More are born than can survive.** Given that, individuals with any advantage, "however slight," have the best chance of surviving and of procreating their kind — while "any variation in the least degree injurious would be rigidly destroyed."

And then the definition:

> This preservation of favourable variations and the rejection of injurious variations, I call Natural Selection.

Two halves, not one: preservation *and* rejection. It is easy to remember only the first.

Darwin immediately marks off what his principle does **not** do. "Variations neither useful nor injurious would not be affected by natural selection, and would be left a fluctuating element, as perhaps we see in the species called polymorphic." A variation that makes no difference to the battle of life is invisible to selection; it neither accumulates nor is destroyed, but drifts as a fluctuating element. Darwin's theory is not a claim that every character of every organism has been shaped for a purpose.

Note also the argumentative style here. The paragraph is built out of questions — "Can it, then, be thought improbable...?", "can we doubt...?" — that invite the reader to grant each step rather than assert them flatly. Darwin is arguing for the *plausibility* of the mechanism from things his readers already accept: the plasticity of domestic breeds and the potency of the breeder's selection.

## Three culminating themes

### 1. Extinction caused by natural selection

The headnote does not say extinction is caused by climate, or catastrophe, or exhaustion of some inner vitality. It says extinction is **caused by natural selection** — the very same process that produces improvement. This is a striking pairing to sit with. If selection means preservation of the favoured and "rigid destruction" of the less favoured, then the making of new and better-adapted forms and the disappearance of old ones are not two processes but one, seen from two sides.

### 2. Divergence of character

Darwin's second theme is **divergence of character**: the tendency of descendants from one stock to become more and more unlike one another as they change. The headnote does not merely name it, it names the two bodies of evidence Darwin will connect it to:

- **the diversity of inhabitants of any small area** — the fact that a patch of ground supports many different kinds of living thing rather than many copies of one kind;
- **naturalisation** — the behaviour of species introduced into new countries.

These are the observations against which the principle of divergence is to be tested. Keep the pairing in mind; it is the hinge of the later argument.

### 3. The grouping of all organic beings

The last two items belong together: "Action of Natural Selection, through Divergence of Character and Extinction, on the descendants from a common parent. Explains the Grouping of all organic beings."

Here the two preceding themes are combined and applied to one starting point — **descendants from a common parent** — and the result is offered as an explanation of a very general fact about nature: that organisms fall into groups, and groups within groups, rather than being spread evenly across all conceivable forms. Divergence supplies the spreading apart; extinction removes intermediate forms, leaving gaps between what remains. Between them they are meant to account for the pattern that naturalists had long described in classifications but not explained.

## What to carry forward

- Natural selection is defined as preservation of favourable and rejection of injurious variations; neutral variations are simply left fluctuating.
- The chapter promises to derive three large results from that small definition: extinction, divergence of character, and the grouping of organic beings.
- Divergence is to be argued in relation to two specific things: the diversity of inhabitants of a small area, and naturalisation.
- Grouping is explained by divergence and extinction acting *together*, on descendants from a common parent.

The excerpt we have announces these themes; the body of the chapter is the attempt to earn them.
